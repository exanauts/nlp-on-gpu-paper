\section*{Introduction}
We thank the reviewers for their comprehensive feedbacks on the previous
version of our paper. The comments pointed to several limitations.
We have revised the paper accordingly, and we believe that this new version addresses
the shortcomings raised before by the reviewers.
We have addressed one by one the comments made by the two reviewers in
the following sections.

\section{Reviewer 1}
\def\reviewername{Reviewer~\#1}
\bigskip

\begin{quotereviewer}
The GPU linear solver is an extremely optimized piece of software, developed specifically to fit the characteristics of the hardware by the manufacturer itself. To obtain a fair comparison, the CPU tests should be performed in similar circumstances.
The Authors use older linear solvers like MA27/57 instead of more recent ones like MA86/87 or Pardiso. In my experience, the latter have a considerable performance advantage in many cases. Consider that MA57 is not inherently parallel, but only exploits the parallelism coming from BLAS. MA27 even predates BLAS and so effectively it is not a parallel code. Comparing a serial code with a massively parallel one, like cuDSS, does not seem fair.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
The reviewer is right that the previous comparison was not fair. We used HSL MA27/HSL MA57 as most
users are using these two linear solvers by default and they are available in Ipopt or Knitro.
We have complemented the numerical results by providing a comparison with Panua Pardiso and HSL MA86, both running in parallel on the CPU.
The results indicate that
(i) the parallel CPU solvers can provide decent speed-ups when they are used within condensed KKT systems (LiftedKKT or HyKKT).
In this regard, LiftedKKT and HyKKT are not only GPU-friendly methods, but is more of a general parallel solver-friendly KKT system reformulation.
In addition, (ii) the performance of the GPU solver cuDSS is still better than the CPU solvers.
In partiuclar, we have found that setting the number of threads to 8 gives the best performance in Pardiso and in HSL MA86, the parallel
scalability remaining limited on the CPU.
This comes from the fact that GPU parallelism is more effective at handling the sparse linear systems arising from many nonlinear program instances (e.g. OPF problems). 

\begin{quotereviewer}
I understand that the Authors want to compare with a common situation in nonlinear programming (i.e. MA57 with OpenBLAS), but the use of a better linear solver and a better BLAS library may have a large effect on the CPU performance. It may even be that the best performance on a CPU is comparable with the speed up observed on the GPU, in some cases.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer For OPF problems, it is known that HSL MA27 gives better performance than
HSL MA57, as the resulting KKT systems are highly sparse \cite{tasseffExploringBenefitsLinear2019}.
Specifically, for the largest instance {\tt case78484epigrids}, the condensed KKT system has ~10 nonzeros per row.
Due to such extreme sparsity, there is little parallelism to exploit at the BLAS level.
As a result, HSL MA57 is not able
to exploit the efficient BLAS kernels it uses under the hood. Changing the BLAS backend (e.g. to MKL)
does not make a significant difference in HSL MA57's performance.
To be more specific, if we take our largest OPF instance ({\tt case78484epigrids}),
the time spent in the numerical factorization of the augmented KKT system
is approximately 1.9s using 1 thread, and does not decrease if we increase
the number of threads (this observation is consistent whether we use openblas or MKL as backend).
This has been clarified in \S6.1.4 in the revision.


\begin{quotereviewer}
The condensed KKT system is denser than the original Hessian $W$ and could potentially be much denser. It is not clear why the Authors say that this phenomenon is ``rarer that in the normal equations commonly encountered in LP' (page 8). For the purpose of using IPMs on GPUs, this approach makes sense because it avoids pivoting. However, the issues mentioned for approach $K_1$ remain valid, limiting the applicability of the technique. This is even more problematic when the term $G^TG$ is added as well.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer The reviewer is right to point this issue.
In particular, the condenstation strategy can lead to a fully dense KKT system if the Jacobian has a fully dense row (similarly to the normal equations being fully dense if the Jacobian has a fully dense column).
We have updated the numerical
results to replace the COPS benchmark by the CUTEst benchmark, and have included a thorough
discussion of the limits faced by the two condensation strategies LiftedKKT and HyKKT,
including the issue of dense columns. This issue arises frequently when solving
optimal control instances, as the time step variable often appears simultaneously in all the
equality constraints (encoding the discretization of the dynamics).


\begin{quotereviewer}
It is not clear how the regularization terms $\delta_c$ and $\delta_w$ are chosen. Page 10 mentions that they are chosen dynamically, but they cannot be chosen during the factorization, as this is done with a black-box solver. So what exactly does this mean? Also, choosing the minimum regularization that makes the matrix positive definite may not be enough to guarantee that Cholesky does not break down, since IPM matrices are extremely ill conditioned. What kind of safeguards are in place?
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer The reviewer is right, and the issue is even more complicated
if the solver enters into feasibility restoration (in that case the matrix appearing
in the (2, 2) block becomes not trivial, as illustrated in \cite[Eq (32)]{wachter2006implementation}.
We have modified the Section 2.2 and Section 3.1 to detail exactly how the regularization
terms are computed. In addition, we have added a new Remark 1 to explain how we solve
the linear system if the dual regularization is positive.


\begin{quotereviewer}
It is not completely clear how to choose the parameter $\gamma$. It should be large enough for numerical reasons, but it risks hiding important information coming from the IPM in the linear system. The theoretical results assume that $\gamma$ is proportional to the reciprocal of the duality gap $\Xi^{-1}$; in practice, $\gamma$ seems to be fixed to a large number. Is there the risk that this produces inaccurate solutions of the linear system in the early stage of the algorithm?
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
One of the shortcoming of the HyKKT method is that we don't know a priori how large should $\gamma$ be. For some problems with a
reduced Hessian being close to singularity, $\gamma$ has to be increased significantly (this is observed for instance in the {\tt ROCKET} instance in CUTEst, as the problem has
a singular arc that leads to a near degenerate reduced Hessian).
We note the parameter $\gamma$ can be tuned adaptively, e.g. using an augmented Lagrangian algorithm: investigating this idea is left for future work.

In this article, we have set $\gamma$ to a relatively high value $\gamma = 10^7$. In practice, this is not an issue in the IPM algorithm.
In the early stage of the algorithm, the solution of the linear system does not have to be very accurate to find the next iterate, as observed e.g. in \cite{zanetti2023new}.
In the latter stage, the ill-conditioning induced by $\gamma$ remains limited compared to the ill-conditioning induced by the active slack variables (all converging to $0$, leading
to terms increasing to $+\infty$ on the diagonal of the KKT system).



\begin{quotereviewer}
Page 23, table 1. Why is the accuracy so much worse when using cuDSS-LDLT? Also, why is the analyse phase so expensive compared to the factorise phase? Does it include the assembly of the matrix as well?
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
cuDSS-LDLT is found to have a lower accuracy than cuDSS-Cholesky, but we do not know why it is the case, as cuDSS is not an open-source library. Analysis phase does not include the assembly of the KKT system, but it is used primarily to compute the symbolic factorization of the KKT system. This includes the ordering of the KKT system and the formation of elimination trees. According to cuDSS documentation, part of the symbolic analysis is run on the CPU \cite{nvidiaNVIDIACuDSSPreview}, as the ordering operation is serial in nature. Thus, analysis phase is often the bottleneck, and this is also observed in the numerical results in Table 1. This discussion is added to the revised version of the paper.

\begin{quotereviewer}
Page 24, table 2. Why does it take fewer IPM iterations to solve the problem to a better accuracy?
Also, what does the last column represent? The accuracy of what? Similar question for Figure 2. Why is the accuracy so much worse when using HyKKT?
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer When using the equality relaxation strategy, we relax the equality constraints $g(x) = 0$ as
a doubly-bounded inequality constraints $-\tau \leq g(x) \leq \tau$, with $\tau$ a small
regularization parameter. The parameter $\tau$ is set equal to the convergence tolerance {\tt tol}
in MadNLP. Hence, every time we change the tolerance, we solve a slightly different optimization problem,
explaining the difference we observe in term of number of iterations.
This has been clarified in \S 6.1.3.


\begin{quotereviewer}
Page 27, Figure 3. From the performance profile, it looks like MA27 is able to solve a larger number of problems.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer We confirm that currently, the linear solvers based on the condensed KKT systems are less robust than
the classical approach solving the augmented KKT system with HSL MA27/MA57.
Our approach is still fairly new, but we have good hope it will improve in the future
as sparse linear solvers get better on the GPU.
We detail in \S 6.2 the instances where the two condensed space approaches LiftedKKT
and HyKKT are failing, and explain why the failures are occuring.


\begin{quotereviewer}
Overall, the issue of accuracy (which is thoroughly discussed theoretically) should be discussed better for the numerical results. This is a crucial aspect for the usability of an interior point solver.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer We have included a new paragraph discussing the accuracy issue in HyKKT/LiftedKKT.

\begin{quotereviewer}
Cholmod seems to perform well in Figure 2, better than MA27. Why are the rest of the tests performed only with MA27 then? It would make a lot of sense to compare to Cholmod as well, since it is not doing pivoting.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer In figure 2, HSL MA27 is used as a reference: it factorizes
the augmented KKT system (which is sparse symmetric indefinite), as done in standard solvers like Ipopt and Knitro.
CHOLMOD factorizes the condensed KKT system we obtain with HyKKT (which on its end is sparse symmetric definite).
CHOLMOD cannot be used for factorizing augmented KKT system due to the indefiniteness.
This has been clarified in the caption of Figure 2.
In addition we have included additional linear solvers running in parallel
on the CPU (Panua Pardiso and HSL MA86), for the benchmark to be more comprehensive.

For the OPF instances, MA27 outperforms MA57, as mentioned in Page 25. This is quite telling of the unusual characteristics of these problems. \section{Reviewer 3}
\def\reviewername{Reviewer~\#3}

\begin{quotereviewer}
1. One major concern is that the experiments are limited to speciﬁc classes of problems, such as
ACOPF, COPS, and PGLIB. While these are certainly important classes of NLP problems,
they are not suﬃciently representative to draw generalized conclusions for all NLP problems.
If the authors wish to make claims for NLP in general, a broader set of experiments involving
benchmarks like CUTEst and Mittelmann is required.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
We have taken into account the remark of \reviewername. The previous version
of the paper was studying both OPF problems (which are known to be super sparse)
and COPS instances (coming from discretized problems). To have a better
overview of the condensed space methods, we have replaced the COPS benchmark
by CUTEst (selecting all the large-scale instances with more than 1,000
variables and at least 1 constraints).

The CUTEst benchmark requires a SIF decoder, here wrapped by the package CUTEst.jl. Once decoded,
the instances are compiled using {\tt gfortran} in a shared library we can
call through CUTEst.jl. As a result, it is impossible to evaluate the model
(including first and second-order derivatives) on the GPU. To alleviate this issue,
we have written a wrapper evaluating the CUTEst instances on the CPU and moving
the results to the GPU with a copy operation. As a result, the method is not running exclusively on the GPU,
as it is the case with the OPF instances (and previously with the COPS instances which
we have all re-implemented using ExaModels).

\begin{quotereviewer}
2.a. The use of condensed-space IPM with GPU acceleration for OPF has already been
studied in the authors’ previous work [37]. It is unclear how the contributions of this
paper diﬀer signiﬁcantly from that prior work.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
Our previous work [37] studied only the solution of large-scale OPF instances
using LiftedKKT,
whereas this article provides a more systematic comparison of the condensed
space approaches LiftedKKT and HyKKT. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item To the best of our knowledge, MadNLP using the two condensed space methods
    (LiftedKKT and HyKKT) is the first GPU-only nonlinear optimization solver.
  \item We use the newly released sparse linear solver
    cuDSS to solve large-scale nonlinear instances using our condensed-space methods.
    Our previous work [37] was using the older linear solver {\tt cusolverRF}.
  \item Despite the increased ill-conditioning caused by the condensed space approaches,
    we provide a theoretical analysis that shows that the ill-conditioning remains
    structured and does not impact the convergence of the interior-point method.
  \item We provide a thorough assessment of LiftedKKT's and HyKKT's, with a comparison
    with state-of-the-art linear solvers running on the CPU.
\end{enumerate}
We have clarified our contributions in the introduction.

\begin{quotereviewer}
2.b. The results presented in Table 4 for a limited number of COPS instances do not paint
a consistent picture, making it unclear what the central message is. Is the claim that
cuDSS or GPU does not oﬀer clear advantages over CPU-based methods for general
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
We have followed \reviewername suggestion and have replaced the COPS benchmark
by CUTEst, selecting only instances with more than 1,000 variables
and at least 1 constraint. This gives a more comprehensive picture of the
performance offered by the condensed-space approaches.
Our claim is not that cuDSS or GPU are offering a clear advantages over CPU-based
method, but rather than they provide a valid alternative for certain large-scale instances
(as shown by the speed-up we obtain on OPF instances or a subset of the CUTEst instances, illustrated in Table 4).

\begin{quotereviewer}
2.c. The observation that GPU performance depends signiﬁcantly on the sparsity pattern of
the matrix is expected. However, the scientiﬁc contribution of this observation is not
apparent and needs further justiﬁcation.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
We have removed the figure displaying the sparsity pattern of the COPS instance,
as it was more confusing than anything else. We have replaced it by the Table 4, which
show the number of variables, constraints and nonzeroes in the Jacobian and Hessian
on the most difficult CUTEst instances.

\begin{quotereviewer}
3. In the context of NLP interior-point methods, the evaluation of Hessian and gradient information often represents a signiﬁcant portion of the overall runtime. However, this aspect is
not discussed in the paper, despite the general claims made about NLP.
\end{quotereviewer}

\answer
We agree with the reviewer that the evaluation of the Hessian and the Jacobian
can take a significant portion of the overall runtime. However, we have observed that
if we can accelerate the derivatives on the GPU using ExaModels, the time spent in
the evaluation becomes negligible compared to the time spent solving the KKT
system (on the largest OPF instances {\tt 78481epigrids}, the AD time
decreases from 7.8s on the CPU to 0.4s on the GPU). This has been clarified in Figure 1 in the revision.

To clarify this point, we have included the time spent in AD in the OPF
and in the CUTest benchmarks. The OPF benchmark uses ExaModels, whereas
the CUTEst benchmark depends on the package CUTEst.jl which evaluates the
derivatives on the CPU. We have also included a new Section 5 that covers more
in depth the implementation we are using, including a new paragraph on the time
spent evaluating the derivatives of nonlinear programs.
