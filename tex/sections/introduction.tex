\section{Introduction}
Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) have become a cornerstone of scientific computing, with their most prominent success being in the training and deployment of large-scale artificial intelligence (AI) models.
GPUs offer two main advantages: (1) massive parallel computing capabilities, especially for applications that exploit coarse-grain parallelism and high memory bandwidth, and (2) superior power efficiency through the use of ``Single Instruction, Multiple Data'' (SIMD) execution.

Despite their success in machine learning, GPUs have seen limited adoption in the mathematical programming community.
This is largely due to the reliance of second-order optimization methods on direct linear algebra to compute Newton directions, a task that remains difficult to parallelize efficiently.
Sparse matrix factorizations, central to these methods, pose significant challenges on SIMD architectures.
However, several recent developments are shifting this landscape:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Faster sparse matrix operations}: Improvements in CUDA libraries and the integration of tensor cores in modern GPUs have significantly accelerated sparse matrix operations~\cite{markidis2018nvidia}.
  \item \textbf{Growing interest in batched optimization}: Applications such as differentiable programming increasingly rely on solving parametric optimization problems in batch mode~\cite{amos2017optnet,pineda2022theseus}.
  \item \textbf{Progress in automatic differentiation}: GPUs are well-suited for automatic differentiation, a key component of both scientific simulations and machine learning~\cite{jax2018github,enzyme2021}. Engineering problems often exhibit recurring patterns throughout the model. Once these patterns are identified, they can be evaluated in parallel within a SIMD framework, enabling near speed-of-light performance~\cite{shin2023accelerating}.
  \item \textbf{Rise of GPU-dominated HPC}: With the emergence of exascale supercomputers (e.g., Frontier, Aurora), GPU support has become a core requirement for scalable scientific software.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Solving optimization problems on GPUs}
These trends have renewed interest in solving optimization problems directly on GPUs.
We now review the state of the art in this area, from first-order methods to recent advances in second-order techniques.

\paragraph{First-order methods for large-scale optimization.}
Sparse matrix factorizations remain a bottleneck on GPUs. For this reason, early efforts focused on first-order methods, which mainly require (sparse) matrix-vector operations, well-suited to GPU execution.
These methods trade precision for scalability and have been shown to outperform even commercial solvers like Gurobi on large-scale linear programs when fully GPU-accelerated~\cite{lu2023cupdlp,lu2023cupdlp2}.

\paragraph{Batched optimization solvers.}
In machine learning, embedding optimization problems into neural networks requires solving many small problems in parallel.
This has led to GPU-native batched solvers capable of handling thousands of small problems simultaneously~\cite{amos2017optnet,pineda2022theseus}.
Similar ideas have been applied to decomposition methods in large-scale optimization~\cite{kimLeveragingGPUBatching2021}.
However, extending these methods to large-scale problems remains difficult, as current implementations often rely on dense linear algebra.

\paragraph{Second-order methods for large-scale optimization.}
First-order methods are well suited to large-scale convex optimization, but extending their success to general nonlinear programming remains challenging. Many real-world engineering problems, such as those in power systems or robotics, involve complex, nonconvex constraints that break any underlying convex structure. In these settings, first-order approaches often struggle to achieve high accuracy. For instance, ADMM fails to converge reliably below a tolerance of $10^{-3}$ on the AC optimal power flow (OPF) problem~\cite{kimLeveragingGPUBatching2021}.

Second-order methods offer a more robust alternative. By solving a Newton system at each iteration, they can provide fast local convergence and high accuracy, even in the presence of nonconvexities. However, these methods rely on solving large, sparse linear systems—typically Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) systems—which poses significant challenges for GPU acceleration.

Historically, GPU-based sparse solvers have lagged behind their CPU counterparts, both in performance and robustness~\cite{swirydowicz2021linear,tasseff2019exploring}.
This gap is now narrowing. In November 2023, NVIDIA released {\tt cuDSS}, a new sparse direct solver providing efficient sparse factorizations with significantly improved performance over earlier GPU-based solvers.
Our benchmarks confirm that {\tt cuDSS} outperforms previously published results on GPU sparse solvers~\cite{shin2023accelerating}.

These advances, combined with recent developments in automatic differentiation and interior-point methods that avoid numerical pivoting, enable robust and scalable second-order methods fully on GPU. In particular, coupling {\tt cuDSS} with a GPU-native AD engine allows us to solve large-scale nonlinear problems like OPF up to 10× faster than previous state-of-the-art methods~\cite{shin2023accelerating}.

Beyond direct solvers, alternative strategies can be used to exploit GPU parallelism in solving KKT systems:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Krylov subspace methods require only matrix-vector products and are inherently parallelizable. However, their use in interior-point methods is complicated by the poor conditioning of KKT matrices. Successful applications typically require careful reformulation and sophisticated preconditioners~\cite{curtisNoteImplementationInteriorpoint2012,rodriguezScalablePreconditioningBlockstructured2020}. Promising results have been obtained in convex settings~\cite{ghannad2022linear}, while nonconvex problems often rely on Augmented Lagrangian reformulations~\cite{cao2016augmented}.
  \item A recent example is the hybrid Golub-Greif method, adapted in~\cite{regev2023hykkt} to solve KKT systems arising in interior-point methods on GPU, with encouraging performance.
  \item Null-space methods (also known as reduced-Hessian methods) eliminate equality constraints to reduce the KKT system to a dense problem, which maps well to GPU architectures. Our previous work has shown that this approach can be highly effective for interior-point methods, provided the number of degrees of freedom remains moderate~\cite{pacaud2022condensed}.
\end{itemize}
These developments open the door to fully GPU-resident second-order solvers that are both robust and performant, even on challenging nonconvex problems.

\add{
\paragraph{A fully GPU-based stack for  large-scale nonlinear optimization.}
Solving constrained, large-scale nonlinear optimization problems on GPUs requires tight coordination between three key components:
(i) a high-level modeling interface,
(ii) a nonlinear optimization solver,
(iii) a sparse linear solver.

To fully benefit from the computational power of GPUs, the entire optimization pipeline must remain on the device, from model evaluation to linear system solves.
It avoids costly transfers between host (CPU) and device (GPU) memory.
This level of integration demands that all components be GPU-compatible and operate natively in GPU memory.

While new architectures such as NVIDIA's GH200 offer unified memory and faster CPU–GPU communication, they still incur slowdowns compared to a fully GPU-resident approach, which we target.

In the classical CPU-based setting, a standard stack for nonlinear optimization consists of using AMPL~\cite{fourer1990ampl} for modeling, Ipopt for the solver, and MA57 (or any other HSL package) for sparse factorization.
Our Julia-based GPU-native ecosystem mirrors this architecture with ExaModels.jl, MadNLP.jl, and {\tt cuDSS}.
% ExaModels.jl provides a symbolic modeling front-end with sparsity detection, MadNLP.jl implements a primal-dual interior-point algorithm with GPU-accelerated automatic differentiation, and cuDSS.jl performs the sparse Cholesky factorizations on the GPU.
This integrated toolchain achieves a fully GPU-resident workflow for solving large-scale nonlinear programs, offering substantial performance gains by minimizing memory transfers and leveraging GPU-optimized kernels across all stages.
If any component is not GPU-aware, it introduces costly CPU–GPU communication, similar to double-buffering in MPI-based distributed systems.

This paper focuses primarily on the central component, the nonlinear optimization solver.
While ExaModels.jl delivers significant speedups, its primary goal is not to replace full-featured modeling frameworks like AMPL, which offer rich syntax, extensive modeling capabilities, and native support for partial separability.
Instead, ExaModels.jl follows a different philosophy: it favors symbolic model construction and native GPU compatibility over maximal generality.
To ensure that models can be evaluated entirely on the GPU, without CPU-GPU memory transfers, we restrict modeling features to those efficiently supported by GPU kernels.
This design choice leads to a smaller and more homogeneous test set compared to frameworks like CUTEst, but ensures consistency and performance across both CPU and GPU execution.
In that sense, ExaModels.jl shares more in common with modern tools such as Gravity~\cite{hijazi2018gravity}.
We expect further progress in parallelism and generic model support in the future, particularly through expression tree analysis.
But as with GPU-based sparse linear solvers and optimization algorithms, each component must be carefully redesigned to fully exploit GPU architectures.

\subsection{Contributions}
In this article, we \add{assess the performance of a filter line-search
interior-point method on modern GPUs. Our aim is
to solve large-scale nonconvex nonlinear programs to optimality.}
We focus on the two condensed-space methods
introduced in~\cite{regev2023hykkt,shin2023accelerating}.
We implement both algorithms inside the GPU-accelerated solver MadNLP,
and leverage the GPU-accelerated automatic differentiation
backend ExaModels~\cite{shin2023accelerating} \add{to accelerate the evaluation
of the derivatives on the GPU}.
\add{
Our main contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item We replace the linear solver {\tt cusolverRF} used in~\cite{shin2023accelerating} with NVIDIA's new {\tt cuDSS} library, enabling GPU-accelerated sparse Cholesky and \ldlt factorization. As a result, the interior-point method is executed entirely on the GPU, from model evaluation to KKT system solve.

\item We extend classical results from~\cite{wright1998ill} to show that the condensed KKT matrices are structurally ill-conditioned in a controlled manner. This yields accurate error bounds for the Schur complement used in the HyKKT method.

\item We conduct a comparative study of the two condensed-space methods, analyzing their accuracy and runtime performance. We go beyond traditional OPF test cases by including large-scale problems from the CUTEst benchmark~\cite{gould2015cutest}.
\end{enumerate}
}
Our findings demonstrate that the condensed-space IPM enables a remarkable ten-time acceleration in solving large-scale OPF instances when utilizing the GPU.
However, performance outcomes on the \add{CUTEst} benchmark exhibit more variability.

\subsection{Notations}
By default, the norm $\|\cdot\|$ refers to the 2-norm.
We define the conditioning of a matrix $A$ as
$\cond(A) = \| A \| \|A^{-1} \|$. % Sungho: I wonder if subscript 2 is necessary in that it is clear from the context that we're connsidering 2 norm.
For any real number $x$, we denote by $\widehat{x}$ its floating
point representation.
We denote $\epstol$ as the smallest positive number such that
$\widehat{x} \leq (1 + \tau) x$ for $|\tau| < \epstol$.
In double precision, $\epstol = 1.1 \times 10^{-16}$.
We use the following notations to proceed with our error analysis.
For $p \in \mathbb{N}$ and a positive variable $h$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We write $x = O(h^p)$ if there exists a constant $b > 0$
    such that $\| x \| \leq b h^p$;
  \item We write $x = \Omega(h^p)$ if there exists a constant $a > 0$
    such that $\| x \| \geq a h^p$;
  \item We write $x = \Theta(h^p)$ if there exists two constants $0 < a < b$
    such that $a h^p \leq \| x \| \leq b h^p$.
\end{itemize}
